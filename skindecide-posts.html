Retinol vs Retinoids — The Hierarchy Nobody Explains

Mon Jan 12 Carousel · 4 slides
01/04 · Active Ingredients

family tree.
Tretinoin

Retinaldehyde

Retinol

Stronger → ↑ Evidence · ↑ Irritation potential
Full Caption
Retinol and retinoids are not the same thing. They're in the same family — but the difference matters. Here's the hierarchy, from weakest to strongest: Retinol Esters (retinyl palmitate) → Retinol → Retinaldehyde → Tretinoin (retinoic acid) Each step requires conversion by your skin. More conversion steps = weaker final effect = less irritation = slower results. Tretinoin (retinoic acid) is the gold standard. It's the pre-converted, biologically active form. Proven in dozens of independent RCTs for acne, photoageing, and hyperpigmentation. Prescription only in most countries. Retinol (over-the-counter): effective, but takes longer. Start at 0.025–0.1%. Introduce slowly — 2×/week for 4 weeks before increasing. Retinaldehyde: middle ground. Stronger than retinol, available OTC, less studied than tretinoin. The hierarchy is about evidence AND tolerance. Pick your starting point based on your skin, not the marketing. SkinDecide detects which retinoid is in your product. Link in bio.

There are 3 types of hyaluronic acid. Most brands use the cheapest one — and don't tell you. HIGH molecular weight HA: Large molecule. Stays on the skin's surface. What to look for: → "Multi-weight hyaluronic acid" claimed on packaging (ask if it's certified) → Multiple HA forms listed: Sodium Hyaluronate + Hydrolyzed Hyaluronic Acid → Actual MW disclosure in the brand's technical documents SkinDecide flags which HA form is in your serum. AHA vs BHA vs PHA — The Exfoliant Breakdown

Mon Jan 19Carousel · 5 slides
01/05 · Exfoliant Science
AHA · BHA · PHA
The exfoliant hierarchy.
AHA
Glycolic, Lactic · Surface · Water-soluble
BHA
Salicylic · Pores · Oil-soluble
PHA
Gluconolactone · Gentle · Large molecule
pH 3.5–4.0 = optimal · Below 3.5 = irritating
Full Caption
Three types of chemical exfoliants. One right one for your skin. Here's the breakdown. AHA (Alpha Hydroxy Acids) Water-soluble. Work on the skin's surface. Break the bonds between dead skin cells. Best for: dry skin, dull texture, hyperpigmentation. Key examples: Glycolic acid (smallest, strongest), Lactic acid (gentler, hydrating). BHA (Beta Hydroxy Acids) Oil-soluble. Penetrate into pores. Dissolve sebum and dead cells from inside. Best for: oily skin, blackheads, acne-prone. The only cosmetically approved BHA: Salicylic acid. PHA (Polyhydroxy Acids) Large molecule — can't penetrate deeply. The gentlest option. Minimal irritation. Best for: sensitive skin, rosacea, beginners. Bonus: antioxidant properties. The critical detail: pH matters. — Below pH 3.5: effective but highly irritating — pH 3.5–4.0: clinical sweet spot — Above pH 4.5: minimal exfoliating effect Don't layer acids without understanding what you're stacking. Vitamin C: Why Your Serum Might Be Doing Nothing

Fri Jan 23Reel · 30s
INCI Spotlight · Vol. 003
Vitamin C
L-Ascorbic Acid · Unstable · pH-critical
⚠ Stability issues
Active at pH <3.5 only
Degrades in light + air
Orange colour = oxidised = inactive
L-Ascorbic Acid
Strong evidence
Unstable
Ascorbyl Glucoside
Less evidence
More stable
Dark glass packaging = shelf life. Clear packaging = marketing.
Full Caption
Your Vitamin C serum might be doing nothing. Here's how to tell. L-Ascorbic Acid is the only form of Vitamin C with strong, independent clinical evidence for: → Brightening (inhibiting melanin synthesis) → Antioxidant protection (neutralising UV-induced free radicals) → Collagen synthesis (cofactor for hydroxylation) The problem: it's inherently unstable. Three things destroy it. 1. pH: L-AA only works at pH below 3.5. Most "gentle" formulations use pH 4.5–5 = no effect. 2. Light: UV degrades L-AA within weeks. Clear packaging accelerates this. You want dark glass or airless pump. 3. Air: Oxidation. Once you open the bottle, oxidation begins. A properly stored serum lasts ~3 months open. How to know if your serum has gone off: It turns orange or brown. That's oxidised ascorbic acid. Bin it. Vitamin C derivatives (Ascorbyl Glucoside, Magnesium Ascorbyl Phosphate): more stable, less irritating, but significantly less evidence than pure L-AA. Full Caption
Your skin barrier is the stratum corneum — the outermost layer of your skin, made of dead cells held together by a lipid matrix (ceramides, cholesterol, free fatty acids). Think: bricks and mortar. Your skin is the brick. The lipid matrix is the mortar. When the mortar breaks down, water escapes (TEWL — transepidermal water loss), and irritants get in. Tight, dry, reactive skin? Compromised barrier. What breaks it: — Harsh surfactants (SLS, high-pH cleansers) — Over-exfoliation (daily acids without recovery time) — Fragrance (disrupts lipid matrix) — Hot water (strips lipids) — pH mismatch (healthy skin is pH 4.5–5.5 — most bar soaps are pH 9–11) What repairs it: — Ceramides (specifically NP, AP, EOP) — Cholesterol (structural lipid, often missing from products) — Free fatty acids (linoleic acid, oleic acid) — Petrolatum (most evidence-backed occlusive — prevents water loss while barrier repairs) The gold standard ratio: Ceramide:Cholesterol:Fatty acid = 3:1:1 — mimics the natural lipid matrix. "Barrier repair" products without these ingredients are selling you the concept, not the mechanism. SkinDecide identifies barrier-supporting ingredients in your moisturiser. Link in bio.

The Evidence Hierarchy: What Kind of Proof Actually Means Something in Skincare

Mon Feb 2Carousel · 5 slides
01/05 · Evidence Science
Not all
evidence is
equal.
HighestIndependent RCT · Peer-reviewed journal
MediumSystematic review of smaller studies
LowIn-vitro / single case study
None"Our lab results"
Tretinoin: highest tier. Most "serums": bottom tier.
Full Caption
Not all skincare evidence is the same. The industry counts on you not knowing the difference. The evidence hierarchy (highest to lowest): 1. Independent RCT (Randomised Controlled Trial) Participants randomly assigned to treatment or placebo. Blind or double-blind. Published in a peer-reviewed journal. Reproducible. This is the gold standard. Tretinoin has dozens. Niacinamide has many. Most serums have zero. 2. Systematic review / meta-analysis Combines results of multiple smaller studies. Stronger than any single study, weaker than a large independent RCT. 3. Single controlled study (not randomised) Useful, but lacks the rigour to establish causation. 4. In-vitro study In a lab, on cells, not on human skin. Not the same as working on you. 5. "Our lab results" / Brand-funded testing Unpublished. No peer review. Statistically convenient. Legally allowed. Scientifically meaningless as proof. When a brand says "clinically proven": ask which level. They almost never say. Full Caption
Bakuchiol is not "natural retinol." It's a meroterpene from the Psoralea corylifolia plant. Different molecule. Different mechanism. Different evidence base. What it does: Some research shows it upregulates retinol-responsive genes — similar pathways to retinol, without being retinol. It also has antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. The evidence: There are now 3 independent RCTs. One directly compared it to 0.5% retinol and found comparable results at 12 weeks for fine lines and pigmentation. That's meaningful — but 3 RCTs vs 60+ years of retinol research is not a tie. Why people choose it: → Less irritating (no retinoid dermatitis) → No photosensitivity (can be used AM and PM) → Often marketed as pregnancy-safe (not formally approved, consult your doctor) Why it's not a retinol replacement: → Far less evidence for acne → No evidence for severe photoageing → No prescription-strength equivalent Use bakuchiol if retinol irritates you. Don't use it thinking it's the same thing. SkinDecide flags both and shows the evidence level comparison. Link in bio.
